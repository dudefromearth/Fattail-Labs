# Lesson 1 — What an option is actually for

*Pure Options · Module 1: See It*

If you've recently finished a course on options, you can probably already list the
strategies. The covered call. The cash-secured put. The wheel. Assignment, and what to do
when it happens.

And if you've been trading options for a while, you've likely noticed something you couldn't
quite name — the strategies never sat right. You kept adjusting them. Widening strikes,
rolling out, shifting the timing. Small corrections to something that almost fit.

Those two experiences have the same cause. And it isn't that you learned it badly.

### Where the teaching quietly points

Look at what those strategies have in common.

A covered call requires shares you already own. A cash-secured put is a promise to buy
shares, with the cash set aside to prove you mean it. The wheel is a loop between those two.
Assignment is what happens when the shares actually turn up.

Every one of them ends in ownership. The option is never the point — it's the toll you pay
on the way to holding stock, or the rent you collect for agreeing to hold it.

There's a reason education drifts there, and it isn't a conspiracy. Ownership is
comprehensible. Everyone already understands what it means to own a thing, so a strategy
that ends in ownership can be explained to anyone in a single sitting. It also *feels* safe,
because the worst case has a familiar shape — you're left holding shares, which is what most
people came to the market to hold anyway. And a course built that way is easy to sell,
because it promises income from something you were going to buy regardless.

None of that is dishonest. It's a real approach and people run it seriously.

It's just built for a different clock than yours.

### The clock those strategies need

Count the time in them.

A covered call needs weeks for premium to be worth collecting, and it needs you to still own
the shares at the end. A cash-secured put needs the capital parked for the whole term. The
wheel needs *both*, repeatedly, in sequence — it isn't a trade, it's a rotation you commit
to over months.

Every one of those postures assumes you have time to be wrong in. That assumption is doing
enormous work, and it's rarely stated out loud. Wait long enough and a bad entry becomes a
decent one. Collect enough premium and a drawdown gets absorbed. The strategies are built so
that duration is your friend.

### The clock you actually trade

You work in days. Sometimes in hours.

There is no duration to hide in. A position you open this morning resolves before you sleep,
or within the week. Nothing gets absorbed, nothing gets waited out, and nothing gets rescued
by the passage of time — because the passage of time is the thing you're trading against, or
with, deliberately.

So when a long-horizon strategy is handed to a short-horizon trader, it doesn't fail
dramatically. It just never quite fits, and the trader spends years adjusting a tool that was
built for someone else's timeframe.

That's the misfit. Not bad instruction — a mismatched clock.

### What the instrument is actually for

Here's the part that almost nobody opens with.

Every other instrument in the market hands you a payoff you have to accept. Buy a stock and
your outcome is a straight line: price goes up, you make money in direct proportion; price
goes down, you lose in direct proportion. You can choose *whether* to be in it and *how much*
to hold. You cannot choose the shape of what happens next.

An option is different in kind, not degree. It is the only instrument where the shape itself
is yours to build.

You decide, before you enter, the most you can lose. You decide where the position starts
paying and where it stops. You decide whether the outcome is a straight line, a step, or a
tent that pays most in a region you nominate. None of that is discovered afterward. All of it
is set at construction, by choosing which contracts to hold and which to sell against them.

That's what options are for. Not a cheaper way to buy stock. Not rent collected on shares.
A set of components from which you build the payoff you actually want.

**Design your thesis. Design your risk. Design your payoff.**

You form a view. You decide what you're prepared to lose to express it. And you construct a
position whose shape matches the view you started with. Three decisions, all yours, all made
before a dollar is committed.

That's the whole course. Everything after this lesson is learning to do it well.

### What we're setting down

For the rest of this course, four things are out of scope:

- Share ownership
- Assignment
- Covered-call income
- The wheel

Not because they're illegitimate. Because they answer a question you're not asking, on a
clock you don't trade.

And there's a real benefit to setting them down now rather than later. Every one of those
strategies teaches you to think about options in terms of what stock you'll end up holding.
That habit is difficult to unlearn once it's formed, because it quietly shapes how you read
every position afterward. If you never build it, you never have to dismantle it.

You aren't giving anything up here. You're picking up the instrument by the end it was built
for.

### Your drill

Open an option chain on any liquid underlying. Pick any strike, any expiration.

Now write down what you would need to own in order to trade it.

The answer is nothing. Not a share, not a promise to buy shares, not capital parked against
an obligation. The contract stands on its own, and its value comes from what it *is*, not
from what it might turn into.

Sit with that for a second. It's the whole premise of everything that follows.
