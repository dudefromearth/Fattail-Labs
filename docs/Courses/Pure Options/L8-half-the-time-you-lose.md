# Lesson 8 — Half the time you lose. That's the design

*Pure Options · Module 3: Run It*

You can build a structure and screen for one worth building. Now the part most courses leave
out: what actually happens when you deploy it, repeatedly, over time.

The honest version is uncomfortable at first glance and reassuring once you understand it.

**Roughly half the time, price never comes anywhere near your structure, and you lose the
entire debit.**

That's not the method failing. It's the method doing exactly what you designed it to do — and
this lesson is about why that sentence is true rather than a consolation.

### Where price finishes, relative to your structure

Deploy this over and over and outcomes sort into four regions.

**Price never approaches.** Around half the time. The market goes somewhere else, or nowhere,
and your structure sits untouched at its floor. You lose the debit — the number you chose
before you entered.

**Price approaches but doesn't arrive.** Most of the remainder. Price moves toward your
structure without reaching the tent. This region is where the position spends most of its
working life, and it's where the extrinsic value in your short middle strikes does its work.

**Price reaches the tent.** Uncommon.

**Price finishes at the peak.** Rare.

Notice the shape of that. The most likely outcome by a wide margin is the one where you lose.
The rarest is the one that pays most. If your instinct is that this is backwards, that instinct
is the thing this lesson exists to correct.

### Why a structure that usually loses is the right structure

Here's the reframe, and it's the whole point of the design frame this course runs on.

You did not *suffer* that loss. **You chose its size before you entered**, and you chose it
knowing it would happen often. The debit wasn't a bet that went wrong. It was a designed floor
that performed exactly as specified.

Compare it to the alternative from Lesson 5. Selling naked, you'd win most of the time and lose
rarely — and the rare loss would be unbounded. You'd have a comfortable win rate and no idea
what your worst day looks like.

You've taken the opposite deal deliberately: lose often, in an amount you set, and keep the
large outcomes available. **Frequency of winning and quality of outcome are separate things,
and this course optimises the second.** A method that wins most of the time and can't tell you
its worst case has sold you comfort. A method that loses often with a floor you drew yourself
has sold you control.

That's what *design your risk* means in practice. Not risk avoided. Risk specified.

### The part that catches people: management is the trade

Now the thing that changes how you'd read those zones, and it's the single most important
paragraph in this lesson.

**Held to expiration, this structure resolves worthless the overwhelming majority of the
time.**

Not just in the first zone. In the second one too — the approach zone. At expiration, price
finishing below the lower wing means the position is worth nothing at all. The tent only pays
at expiry if price finishes *inside* it, and that's the uncommon-and-rare region combined.

So if you hold every position to the final bell, you experience almost nothing but total
losses.

Where does the gain come from, then?

**From the transit, not the destination.** As price moves toward your structure, the position
inflates — the extrinsic value in your short middle strikes responds, the shape starts pricing
in the possibility of arrival, and what you hold becomes worth more than you paid. That
happens *on the way*, before anything is settled.

You take that. You don't wait for the peak.

This is why Lesson 3 mattered. The smooth line and the hard line are not the same, and the
whole opportunity lives in the distance between them. A trader who only reads the expiration
line sees a structure that almost never pays. A trader who reads both sees one that becomes
sellable long before expiration decides anything.

**The bread-and-butter outcome is exiting outside the tent.** If you're optimising for the
peak, you've misread the structure.

And it means management isn't an optimisation layered on top of this method. It *is* the
method. A butterfly held passively to expiry is a different, much worse trade than the same
butterfly managed on the approach — same structure, same entry, opposite results.

### The uncomfortable corollary

The days when price moves hardest toward your structure are the days management is most
demanding.

Fast transit through a tent. Spreads widening as volatility spikes. Prices moving between the
moment you decide and the moment you act. The same conditions that create the opportunity
degrade the execution, and they arrive together because they're the same event.

That's not a reason to avoid those days. It's a reason to know before one arrives that a plan
made in advance is the only plan you'll actually have. Running this under pressure, in real
time, is what **0DTE Foundations** exists to teach — and it's genuinely a separate skill from
everything in this course.

### What this means for how you judge yourself

If half your positions lose the debit by design, then a losing trade tells you nothing.

Not "almost nothing." Nothing. It's the modal outcome. A run of them is unremarkable. Reading a
single loss as evidence about your method is like reading one coin flip as evidence about the
coin — and traders who do it end up changing a working approach in response to noise, which is
how a method gets abandoned right before it would have worked.

So what *do* you judge?

Whether you followed the screen. Whether you sized correctly. Whether you managed the position
you said you'd manage. Whether you took what the transit offered instead of waiting for the
peak. All process, all knowable immediately, all independent of where price happened to go.

The results themselves only mean anything across many deployments — which is the subject of the
next lesson, and the reason this one had to come first.

### Your drill

No chain for this one. Take a piece of paper.

Write down the debit for a structure you sketched in the last lesson. Then write this
underneath, in your own words:

*If I deployed this ten times, I would expect to lose this amount about five times, and that
would mean the design worked.*

Now sit with whether you actually believe it.

That's not a rhetorical exercise. If some part of you expects to be right more often than that,
this method will feel like failure while it's working, and you'll abandon it during a normal
stretch. Better to find that out on paper than with money on it.

## Notes — not part of the lesson

**Frequencies are qualitative throughout.** Around half, most of the remainder, uncommon, rare.
The measured proportions exist and are more precise, but stating them alongside outcomes turns
the lesson into an expectancy model — and measured returns from real trades carry disclosure
obligations. Qualitative language delivers the whole teaching point with none of that exposure.

**No return figures anywhere in this lesson or its images.** This was the largest invariant-8
exposure in the source material and the lesson is written to be safe by construction rather
than by disclaimer.

**Two gates beyond the usual.** Hotel on the management claim — the assertion that the approach
zone gains on transit and resolves worthless at expiry has to be exactly right. Tango on the
overall tone: the lesson has to leave a reader steadier, not deflated, and the design frame is
what does that work.

**The lineage claim was deliberately left out.** An earlier framing described outcomes as
matching a normal distribution, which contradicts the fat-tail position the house holds
elsewhere. Nothing in this lesson depends on it, so it isn't here.
