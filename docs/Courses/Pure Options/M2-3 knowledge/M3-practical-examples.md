# Practical Examples — Run It

*Pure Options · Module 3*

These examples are different in kind from the ones in Modules 1 and 2, and the difference is the
lesson.

You can't demonstrate Module 3 with a trade. Its subject is what happens across many deployments,
under pressure, over a stretch — so these are **decision scenarios**. Each one puts you at a
moment where something has to be decided, and the decision is the thing being taught.

Work out your answer before reading on. What you decide before seeing the reasoning is the honest
measure.

Same fictional index: **MKT at 5,000.**

---

## Scenario A — The position that went nowhere

You deployed a 35-wide structure, body at 5,095, for a debit of 2.80. Three days have passed of a
five-day term.

MKT is at 4,980. It drifted down, then back, and has spent three days doing very little. Your
structure is priced at 0.60.

**What do you do?**

### The decision

Nothing. This is a Zone 0 outcome in progress — price never approached, and the position is doing
exactly what half of them do.

### Why that's the answer

The temptation is to salvage something. 0.60 is still 0.60, and closing recovers a fraction of
the debit rather than watching it go to zero.

That reasoning isn't wrong, exactly, but it's answering the wrong question. The debit was
committed at entry as a defined, expected loss. Recovering a fifth of it changes very little,
and the habit of closing dead positions early has a cost that shows up elsewhere: it trains you
to intervene, and intervention is what you can least afford when a position is actually moving.

**The more important point is what this scenario should feel like.** If watching 2.80 become 0.60
produces a strong urge to act, that's worth noticing about yourself now rather than during a run
of five of them. Lesson 8's drill asked whether you actually believed half would lose. This is
what believing it looks like in practice.

If your campaign plan specifies closing dead positions at a threshold, follow it. If it doesn't,
this isn't the moment to invent one.

## Scenario B — The approach

Same structure — 35 wide, body at 5,095, paid 2.80. Two days in.

MKT has moved up to 5,050. Your structure is now priced at **6.20**.

Price is still below the lower wing at 5,060. At expiration, right now, this position is worth
nothing.

**What do you do?**

> **Type:** Static, annotated
> **Note:** The single most important image in Module 3. Expiration value zero, current value 6.20, and the entire difference is what's on offer. Same visual language as L3 and L8.

### The decision

You take it, or you take part of it. This is the bread-and-butter outcome.

### Why that's the answer

Look at the two numbers. At expiration this structure is worth **zero** — price is below the
lower wing and would need another 10 points just to reach the region where the tent pays
anything.

Right now it's worth 6.20, against 2.80 paid.

That entire 6.20 is extrinsic value. It exists because price came close enough that the market is
pricing the possibility of arrival — the short body inflated, the shape started to matter, and the
position became worth more than you paid without anything being settled.

**This is what "the gain comes from the transit, not the destination" means.** You are being
offered something now for a position that would pay nothing if the clock stopped. Waiting for
price to reach the tent means giving this back for a chance at more.

A trader reading only the expiration line sees a losing position and holds for the peak. A trader
reading both lines sees the gap and takes it. That's the whole reason Lesson 3 came where it did.

**The discipline here is exiting outside the tent.** If you optimise for the peak, you'll return
most of what the approach zone offers you.

## Scenario C — The violent day

Same structure. One day remaining.

Something happened overnight. MKT gapped and is moving fast — it opened at 5,070, ran to 5,110,
and is still moving. Your structure has gone from 2.80 to 9.00 to 14.00 within twenty minutes.

The bid-ask spread has widened from 0.30 to 1.20. Prices are moving between the moment you look
and the moment you'd act.

**What do you do?**

### The decision

Take what's available, at a price you'll accept, now.

### Why that's the answer

Three things are true simultaneously and they're all consequences of the same event.

**The opportunity is real.** This is the transit at speed, and there is more on offer than
Scenario B provided.

**Execution has degraded.** A 1.20 spread on a structure worth 14.00 is a meaningful fraction of
what you're trying to capture, and it's widening because everyone else is also trying to act.

**Price can pass through your tent and out the other side.** Above 5,130 the structure's
expiration value returns to zero. On a fast day, the distance between "approaching" and "gone
past" is minutes.

The mistake is waiting for a better price during a violent move. There may not be one, and the
window is not stable.

**What this scenario is really teaching:** you can read this and understand it completely, and
still find that executing it while it's happening is a different skill. Twenty minutes, a moving
price, a widening spread, and a decision that has to be made without deliberation — that's
**0DTE Foundations**, and it isn't a formality. This course can tell you what the right decision
is. It cannot give you the practice of making it at speed.

## Scenario D — The quiet stretch

You're eleven deployments into a campaign planned for thirty.

Seven of the eleven have gone to zero. Three returned small amounts on shallow approaches. One
was a good transit. Your speculative allocation is down meaningfully, and the market has been
sitting still for weeks — VIX has drifted from 21 to 15 and stayed there.

**What do you do?**

### The decision

Continue at the planned cadence, with width reduced to reflect the regime, and check the drawdown
against your pause threshold.

### Why that's the answer

Seven losses in eleven is close to what Lesson 8 said to expect, and a quiet market is a market
with little to give a structure that pays on movement. This is the method working in conditions
that don't suit it — which is not the same as the method failing.

Two things do need to change, and both were written down before the campaign started.

**Width follows the regime.** VIX at 15 is Zombieland, so structures narrow to the 20–30 band. The
same width you used at VIX 21 is now the wrong structure.

**The pause threshold gets checked.** Not intuited — checked, against the number you wrote in
advance. If drawdown has reached it, you pause and review. If it hasn't, you continue.

**And one thing must not change: size.** The temptation after seven losses is to deploy larger and
recover faster. You know from Lesson 8 that a loss carries no information about the next
deployment. Sizing up here converts a designed, survivable pattern into a concentrated bet at
exactly the moment your judgment is worst.

**The real question this scenario asks** is whether you can tell a bad regime from a broken
method. The pause threshold exists because you can't reliably tell the difference in the middle
of it — so you decide in advance what level of evidence triggers a proper look, rather than
deciding in the moment when you're eleven deployments in and down.

## Scenario E — The one that worked

One deployment paid substantially. A good transit, taken well, on a day the market moved your way.

You're feeling clear-headed and confident. The method makes sense. You can see how the pieces fit.

**What do you do?**

### The decision

Exactly what the campaign plan says. Same size. Same cadence. Log it and continue.

### Why that's the answer

This scenario is here because it's the one nobody expects to be dangerous.

A single win carries no more information than a single loss. It doesn't validate the method, it
doesn't indicate that conditions have turned, and it isn't evidence that you've got the hang of
something. It's one instance of a distribution you already knew had good outcomes in it.

The failure mode is subtle: confidence after a win produces a slightly larger next position, a
slightly looser screen, a candidate accepted at 11% of width because it "looks like the last one."
None of those feel like breaking the rules. All of them are.

**Size changes at campaign boundaries, from a written rule, based on the retrospective.** Not
after a good day. Especially not after a good day.

---

## What these five have in common

In four of the five, the correct action is to follow a decision made earlier — before the position
existed, before you knew how it would feel.

That's not a coincidence and it isn't a coincidence in live trading either. The campaign plan from
Lesson 9 exists because decisions made during a position are made by someone with a stake in the
answer, and decisions made before it are made by someone who only wants to be right.

**The one scenario where you must decide in the moment is Scenario C** — and that's precisely the
one this course tells you it hasn't taught you to handle.

---

## Your drill

Take your campaign plan from Lesson 9.

Read each scenario above and check: does your plan actually answer it? Not "would I know what to
do" — does the written plan tell you?

Most first drafts answer two or three. If yours doesn't specify what happens on a quiet stretch,
or what triggers a pause, or what you do with a dead position at 20% of debit, add them now.

A plan that only covers the situations you enjoy is not a plan.

## Notes — not part of the lesson

**No cumulative results anywhere.** Scenario D describes a drawdown qualitatively — "down
meaningfully" — with no figure, and no scenario states what a position returned as a percentage or
a total. Prices are shown as structure prices, which are mechanical facts, not performance.

**Scenario E is the one most likely to be cut and shouldn't be.** A worked example after a winning
trade looks redundant, and it's the scenario where members actually break their own rules. Its
inclusion is a Tango point as much as a trading one.

**Scenario C deliberately hands off to Foundations** rather than teaching execution under
pressure. It shows the decision and states plainly that knowing it and doing it are different —
which keeps the course honest about its boundary and makes L10's "cannot yet" list concrete rather
than abstract.

**Scenario A's answer may need a house ruling.** I've written "do nothing" as the default while
deferring to whatever the member's campaign plan specifies. If FatTail practice is to close dead
positions at a threshold, that should be stated as the rule rather than left to the plan — Hotel
should confirm.
