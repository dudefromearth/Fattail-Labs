# Knowledge Check — Run It

*Pure Options · Module 3*

Ten questions. Most test judgment rather than recall — several describe a situation and ask what
follows from it.

Answers follow. Work through all ten first.

---

## Questions

**1.** Roughly how often does price never approach the structure at all?

- A. Rarely
- B. About a quarter of the time
- C. About half the time
- D. Almost never

---

**2.** A butterfly held to expiration, with price finishing below the lower wing, is worth:

- A. Its intrinsic value
- B. Nothing
- C. The debit paid
- D. The width minus the debit

---

**3.** Where does the gain in this method usually come from?

- A. Price finishing at the peak
- B. The position inflating as price approaches, taken before expiration
- C. Time decay on the wings
- D. Volatility expansion

---

**4.** You've had four losing deployments in a row. What does that tell you?

- A. The method is failing
- B. Conditions have changed
- C. Very little — it's within the expected pattern
- D. You should increase size to recover

---

**5.** Why does a campaign specify a *pause* threshold rather than a stop?

- A. Pausing is less disruptive
- B. A run of losses may be a quiet regime rather than a broken method
- C. It allows the position to recover
- D. Stops don't work on butterflies

---

**6.** Why does deploying at a regular cadence matter?

- A. It spreads commission costs
- B. Large moves cluster, and you can't know in advance which sessions will produce them
- C. It reduces average cost
- D. It satisfies the diversification rule

---

**7.** In the barbell, when may the stable side be used to top up the speculative side?

- A. When the speculative side is depleted
- B. When a high-conviction opportunity appears
- C. After a drawdown, up to the original allocation
- D. Never beyond its original allocation

---

**8.** You take a substantial gain on one deployment. What changes about your next one?

- A. Size increases proportionally
- B. Nothing
- C. The screen can be slightly relaxed
- D. Cadence increases while conditions are favourable

---

**9.** When may position size change?

- A. After a losing streak, to recover
- B. After a winning trade, to press the advantage
- C. At campaign boundaries, from a written rule
- D. Whenever conviction is high

---

**10.** On a fast-moving day, your structure has gone from 2.80 to 14.00 and spreads have widened
considerably. What's the primary risk?

- A. That you exit too early
- B. That price passes through the tent while you wait for a better fill
- C. That volatility collapses
- D. That the broker rejects the order

---

## Answers

**1 — C. About half the time.**

Price never comes near the structure in roughly half of deployments, and the debit is lost in
full. That's the modal outcome and it's designed.

*If you chose A or D:* this is the expectation most worth correcting before deploying anything.
A method that surprises you with its normal behaviour will get abandoned during a normal stretch.

---

**2 — B. Nothing.**

Below the lower wing, all four legs expire worthless. The tent only has value at expiration if
price finishes inside it.

This is why the approach zone requires management. At expiration, "approached but didn't arrive"
and "never came close" are the same outcome — total loss.

---

**3 — B. The position inflating as price approaches, taken before expiration.**

The transit, not the destination. Scenario B in the practical examples shows a structure worth
zero at expiration and 6.20 right now — and the 6.20 is the opportunity.

*If you chose A:* the peak is the rare outcome. Optimising for it means returning most of what the
approach offers.

---

**4 — C. Very little — it's within the expected pattern.**

Four losses in a row is unremarkable when roughly half are expected to lose. It carries no
information about the next deployment.

*If you chose D:* that's the instinct the "never increase size after losses" rule exists to
override. It converts a designed, survivable pattern into a concentrated bet at the moment your
judgment is least reliable.

---

**5 — B. A run of losses may be a quiet regime rather than a broken method.**

A quiet market gives a movement-dependent structure very little to work with, and a run of defined
losses in that environment is the method working in unsuitable conditions.

The threshold exists because you can't reliably tell the difference from inside it — so you decide
in advance what evidence triggers a proper review, rather than deciding while you're down.

---

**6 — B. Large moves cluster, and you can't know in advance which sessions will produce them.**

Outsized moves arrive in bursts separated by quiet stretches. Since you can't schedule them,
presence during the quiet is the precondition for being there in the loud.

Irregular deployment means being absent for exactly the sessions the method was built for.

---

**7 — D. Never beyond its original allocation.**

The stable side isn't a reserve to draw on. It's the reason you can take the other side of the
barbell at all.

If the speculative side is spent, the campaign ends. A trader who raids the stable side hasn't
stretched the method — they've dismantled it and kept the name.

---

**8 — B. Nothing.**

A single win carries no more information than a single loss. It doesn't validate the method or
indicate that conditions have turned.

*If you chose A or C:* this is Scenario E, and it's the failure mode nobody expects. Confidence
after a win produces a slightly larger position and a slightly looser screen — neither of which
feels like breaking a rule.

---

**9 — C. At campaign boundaries, from a written rule.**

Based on the retrospective, not on how recent deployments felt. Every other option in this
question describes changing size in response to outcomes, which is the pattern that ends
accounts.

---

**10 — B. That price passes through the tent while you wait for a better fill.**

Above the upper wing, expiration value returns to zero. On a fast day the distance between
"approaching" and "gone past" is minutes, and the spread is widening precisely because everyone
else is trying to act at the same time.

*If you chose A:* exiting early on a violent move costs you some of the available gain. Waiting
can cost all of it.

---

## How you did

**8 or more.** You understand what running this actually involves.

**5 to 7.** Look at which ones you missed. Questions 4, 8 and 9 all test the same discipline from
different directions — if you missed two of those three, re-read Lesson 9's section on sizing.

**Fewer than 5.** Re-read Lessons 8 and 9 and redo the campaign plan drill. The mechanics of this
module are simple; the discipline is not, and the questions are testing the second.

**One diagnostic.** Q4 and Q8 are the same question with opposite signs — one asks what a losing
streak means, the other what a win means. The answer to both is *nothing*. If you answered them
differently, that asymmetry is worth examining, because it's the shape of most trading mistakes.

---

## Notes — not part of the lesson

**Q4 and Q8 are deliberately paired** and separated so the symmetry isn't obvious until the
diagnostic points it out. Members typically answer the loss question correctly and the win
question incorrectly, which is exactly the asymmetry worth surfacing.

**No question asks for a return, a win rate, or a performance figure.** Q1 asks for a frequency of
where price finishes, which is a distributional fact about the market rather than a result.

**Q7 is the barbell's load-bearing rule** and the only question in the course where the correct
answer is an absolute prohibition.

lessons, three knowledge checks.
