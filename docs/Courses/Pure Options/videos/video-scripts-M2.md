## Lesson 5 — Pay the clock, or collect it

**Runtime:** ~65 seconds · **Words:** 138
**Tone:** Building. This lesson moves from observing to choosing, and the delivery should
register that shift.
**Pacing:** Hard stop before "No." after the "so sell everything" question. That single word is
the pivot of the lesson.
**Critical:** the naked-selling section must not sound like a scolding. Some members watching
are doing it right now, possibly with a good record.

### Beats

| Time | Beat | On screen |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00 | Decay is a position, not weather | Decay curve with two arrows, opposite directions |
| 0:15 | The buyer's clock | Declining staircase, underlying flat |
| 0:28 | The seller's clock | Same curve, other side |
| 0:40 | So sell everything? | Hold on speaker |
| 0:45 | The loss line | Line running off the bottom of frame |

### Spoken text

Decay is not weather. It is not something that happens to you. It is a flow with two ends, and
every option trade puts you on one of them.

When you buy, time is a cost you pay every day you hold. Let a day pass with the underlying
completely unchanged, and your position is worth less. Nothing happened, and you were charged
anyway.

When you sell, that reverses. The same day that billed the buyer credits you. And notice what
the seller needs. The buyer needs price to move, far enough, in the right direction, before a
deadline. The seller needs the deadline to arrive.

One of those is uncertain. The other is guaranteed.

So sell everything?

No. And the reason why is the most important thing in this lesson.

## Lesson 6 — The butterfly: two spreads and a tent

**Runtime:** ~70 seconds · **Words:** 145
**Tone:** Constructive, almost hands-on. This is the lesson where the member builds something.
**Pacing:** Let the construction breathe. Each leg is a beat.
**Note:** the visual carries a lot here. The read should feel like narrating an assembly rather
than describing a finished object.

### Beats

| Time | Beat | On screen |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00 | The gap from last lesson | Empty third column |
| 0:10 | Start with a vertical | First spread building |
| 0:22 | Add the second | Second spread, inverted |
| 0:35 | The tent | Full structure, strikes marked |
| 0:48 | Nothing but the debit | Floor highlighted |
| 0:58 | One dial | Same shape, wider and narrower |

### Spoken text

You want time working for you, and a floor under your worst day. No single contract does both.

So you build one.

Take two options, same expiration, different strikes. Buy the nearer, sell the further. That is a
vertical spread, and it already has a floor and a ceiling.

Now do it again, further out, backwards. Sell the nearer, buy the further. Arrange it so both
spreads sold the same strike.

You are now holding four contracts across three strikes, and the shape they make is a tent.

The sold middle harvests the decay. The bought wings cap the damage. And the most you can lose is
what you paid to open it. Not more, in any direction, at any speed.

One more thing about it. When conditions change, you change one number. How wide you build it.
That is all.

## Lesson 7 — Finding one: regime, expected move, price

**Runtime:** ~70 seconds · **Words:** 147
**Tone:** Procedural and unhurried. This is a method being handed over.
**Pacing:** Three clear sections with distinct pauses between them. The member should be able to
hear where one step ends and the next begins.
**Critical:** "or you do not trade" must land as a real instruction, not a caveat.

### Beats

| Time | Beat | On screen |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00 | You do not set the price | Hold on speaker |
| 0:12 | Width from the spectrum | Continuous volatility spectrum |
| 0:30 | Placement at the expected move | Demand thinning past the boundary |
| 0:48 | The price gate | Three candidates, two failing |
| 1:02 | Order matters | Hold on speaker |

### Spoken text

Here is the thing that makes this work. You do not set the price. The chain does.

So your job is not deciding what to trade. It is screening what is on offer. And most days,
nothing on offer qualifies.

Three steps, in this order.

Width comes from volatility. More movement, wider structure. It is a spectrum, not a rule, and
you read it off conditions rather than calculating it.

Placement goes around the expected move. Inside that range is where the crowd is, and dense
demand supports price. Past it, the crowd thins out and the structure gets priced by sellers.

Then price. Between five and ten percent of the width. Not more. Not less. Or you do not trade.

And that order cannot be reversed. Screen by price first and you will buy the thing furthest from
ever happening, every single time.

## Production notes

**Lesson 5's single-word "No" is a real beat.** It needs a genuine stop before it and a short one
after. If HeyGen's pacing runs it into the surrounding sentences, add a line break or a period
sequence in the input and re-render — the pivot of the lesson is in that pause.

**Lesson 6 should sound like assembly.** Each leg added is a separate beat, and the read should
have the rhythm of something being put together in front of the viewer. Avoid a summarising tone
— the tent should feel like it arrived rather than like it was announced.

**Lesson 7 has three numbers to check in the render.** "Five and ten percent" needs to come out
as spoken words rather than symbols, and the sentence structure around it should survive
preprocessing. Listen to that line specifically before approving the render.

**Lesson 5 delivery, again.** The naked-selling material is the most likely place in the course
for a member to feel judged. Warmth in the read is doing real work — the words are already blunt
enough without the tone adding to them.

**None of these three scripts states a number the page does not.** The videos frame the
procedure; the arithmetic lives on the pages and in the practical examples.
